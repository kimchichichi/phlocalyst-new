Chapter 01
Origins
& horns

Phlocalyst was born twice. The first time in Sevilla, to Flemish parents passing through on a long Spanish summer in 1992 — a fact he likes more than he admits when he's the only one in a Munich orchestra with a southern shade of his name. The second time was in 2017, on an iPad, in a studio apartment in Cologne, trying to remake a Pete Rock beat at three in the morning when he should've been asleep before a Mahler rehearsal.

The trumpet came first by a long margin. He picked it up at five, persuaded by a grandfather who'd been a horn player in a village band and never let anyone forget it. By thirteen he was at the conservatory in Antwerp; by twenty he was auditioning in Germany. Classical music was the plan, the practice schedule, the identity. The other stuff — the hip-hop, the jazz, the records — was what he listened to in the car on the way to it.

"I always knew I'd play trumpet. I didn't know there was a second thing until I was already too late to start it."

That second thing arrived sideways. A bandmate handed him an old SP-404 in 2017 — "for stress" — and within a month he was missing rehearsal-mornings because he'd stayed up sampling Robert Glasper through a tape simulator. The first beats were terrible. He kept making them. He started looping his own horn over them and they got less terrible. He gave the new thing a stupid name — "Phlocalyst" — because he didn't want anyone he knew to find it.

↑ Then: a quarantine. ↓

February 2020 — quiet apartment, fever for two days, then five clear ones. He made the whole of Insights in those five days, mostly between meals. He sent it to a friend who sent it to a friend who sent it to Nettwerk, and by the time he'd recovered there was a record deal sitting in his inbox addressed to a name that wasn't his.

Now there are two careers. One pays the rent — third trumpet in a Munich house, Strauss on Thursday, Brahms on Saturday. The other one pays for vinyl pressings, a better SP, and a row of guest beds in Brussels for visiting collaborators. He doesn't think they're the same job. He doesn't think they're different ones either.

In his own words

FROM INTERVIEWS / LINER NOTES
2020 — 2026
My best music is usually made when I'm feeling unwell — there's something about not having the energy to overthink that gets me out of my own way.
On making Insights · Nettwerk press · 2022
The trumpet is louder than anything I own. So whatever I sample, whatever I program — the horn has to be the loudest thing in the room emotionally too, or I won't believe it.
On arranging · The Quietus interview · 2023
Classical music taught me to play long notes. Hip-hop taught me to put them in places long notes are not usually allowed.
On the two careers · Süddeutsche Zeitung · 2024
I don't think the genre is "lo-fi". I think the genre is "music you can read to without it asking anything of you." Sometimes that means it's lo-fi. Sometimes it means it's a five-piece band.
On the label · Bandcamp Daily · 2024
If the snare hits exactly on two and four, I get nervous. If it hits ten milliseconds late, the track relaxes — and I do too.
On rhythm · Tape Op feature · 2025
My grandfather played in the village band for forty years and never recorded a single note. I think about that almost every time I press save.
On legacy · de Standaard · 2025

Things people ask

FAQ · IN ROUGH ORDER OF FREQUENCY
UPDATED MAY 2026
How is "Phlocalyst" pronounced?
Like "flow-ka-list" — the "ph" is silent the way it is in "phenomenon", because the name started as a misread of that word in a notebook. It stayed because nobody else was using it.
Is it actually you on every trumpet take?
Yes. Every horn line on every Phlocalyst release is played by me, with the exception of a single guest verse from Theo Croker on a 2024 compilation. Even the samples that sound like horns — those are also me, just resampled and chopped beyond recognition.
Do you ever play your orchestra job AND a club set on the same day?
Three times so far. Each time I swore I wouldn't do it again. Each time I did it again. The classical playing always suffers more than the beat set — it's harder to fake Strauss tired than it is to fake a chill atmosphere.
What DAW do you use?
Logic Pro since 2017, with most of the actual making happening on a Roland SP-404 MKII before things get arranged in the DAW. The SP is where mistakes turn into songs; Logic is where I clean them up just enough.
Can I license your music?
For sync/film/ads, please write to sync@nettwerk.com — they handle the catalogue. For non-commercial use (school projects, independent short films, charity work) you can write to me directly and the answer is usually yes for free.
Do you take collaborations / features?
Occasionally — usually one to three a year, always with people whose existing music I genuinely listen to. The fastest way to make this happen is not to send a finished track asking for trumpet — it's to send a half-finished one with space in it.
Will you ever release a strictly classical record?
Maybe. There's a piece by Berio I've wanted to record for years. I think about it on every long train ride and then I get home and start a beat instead. So: probably, eventually, but not next.
What's the second LP going to sound like?
More live drums than the first, recorded in a room I rented for two weeks in Cologne with the same engineer who mixed Insights. The trumpet's louder. The reverb is shorter. There's at least one track with no beat at all. That's all I'm going to say until it's finished.
